Voice is having its moment again. Not the "set a timer" kind, but agents that pick up the phone, understand a messy sentence, call a tool, and answer before the silence gets awkward. At Astrolle, a requirement already starts its life as a spoken description, so I've been watching this space closely. Here is where I think it stands right now.

## The money says it's real

In February, ElevenLabs raised a $500M Series D at an $11B valuation, led by Sequoia ([ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/blog/series-d), [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/02/04/elevenlabs-raises-500m-from-sequioia-at-a-11-billion-valuation/)). The company says it closed 2025 at $350M in annual recurring revenue, and the new money goes into ElevenAgents, its platform for voice agents. When a company known for text-to-speech bets its next chapter on agents, that tells you where the demand is.

## Two ways to build one

Every voice agent today is built one of two ways.

**Cascaded.** Speech-to-text, then a language model, then text-to-speech. Each piece can be swapped on its own, the language model is an ordinary text model with mature tool calling, and the cost stays roughly flat per minute. The price is latency: every handoff adds delay before the agent can speak.

**Speech-to-speech.** One model hears and speaks. It can start replying sooner and keeps the tone of the conversation, but you give up modularity. Switching providers means rewriting the streaming and conversation handling.

Neither one wins outright. In [Softcery's comparison](https://softcery.com/lab/ai-voice-agents-real-time-vs-turn-based-tts-stt-architecture) (updated April 2026), leading speech-to-speech models took between 0.78 and 2.98 seconds to first audio, while a turn in human conversation takes around 200 ms. Pricing is all over the place: some speech-to-speech models get more expensive the longer a session runs, while newer ones from Amazon and Google undercut a typical cascaded pipeline. And on a phone line, audio arrives at 8 kHz, where speech-to-speech models tuned for clean web audio lose much of their edge.

## The stack is settling

The tooling has sorted itself into two camps. Managed platforms like Vapi and Retell handle orchestration and telephony for you. Open-source frameworks like LiveKit Agents and Pipecat give you the same pipeline to run on your own infrastructure. Tool calling is now table stakes: OpenAI, Google, xAI, and Amazon all ship it natively in their speech-to-speech models.

## What's still hard

- **Turn-taking.** Knowing when someone has actually finished, and handling it gracefully when they interrupt.
- **Real phone lines.** Accents, background noise, and low-quality audio still break agents that demo perfectly.
- **Testing.** A conversation is much harder to test than an endpoint. Serious teams end up building simulated callers to catch regressions.
- **Trust.** Disclosing that the caller is talking to an AI, getting consent, and following recording laws are product requirements, not afterthoughts.

## What I'd build today

For phone calls, I'd start with a cascaded pipeline: predictable cost, parts you can swap, and a text model whose tool calling you already understand. For voice inside an app or on the web, I'd try speech-to-speech, where the audio is clean and the lower latency shows.

Either way, the lesson is the same one I learned with text agents. Voice is the interface; the hard part is deciding what the agent is allowed to do. At Astrolle, a spoken requirement becomes a plan, an agent implements it with tests, and an engineer approves it before anything becomes a pull request. The voice made it faster to start. The guardrails are what made it safe to ship.
